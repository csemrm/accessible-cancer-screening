\title{Efficient Vision Transformers Match Larger Models on Cervical Cytology Classification, But In-Distribution Accuracy Does Not Predict Domain-Shift Robustness}
\titlerunning{Efficient ViTs Match Larger Models But Miss Domain-Shift Robustness}

\author{Md Mostafizur Rahman}
\authorrunning{M.\ M.\ Rahman}
\institute{San Francisco Bay University \\ \email{csemrm@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Lightweight vision transformers are frequently proposed for medical image
classification on the premise that they trade a small, bounded amount of
accuracy for a large reduction in compute cost, and are rarely tested for
whether that same efficiency holds under the distribution shift a deployed
model will actually encounter across clinics, scanners, and populations.
We benchmark 12 models spanning CNN, full-size vision transformer, and
efficient-vision-transformer families on cervical cytology classification
(SIPaKMeD, 5 Bethesda-relevant cytomorphology classes), then evaluate the
same models zero-shot, with no retraining or recalibration, on two
independent external cytology datasets (Herlev, Mendeley LBC) after
harmonizing all three onto a shared binary normal/abnormal label. Contrary
to the usual efficiency-accuracy trade-off framing, the two most
parameter-efficient models in the entire registry (EfficientViT-M0 and -B0,
$\sim$2.1M parameters each) are also the two best-performing in
distribution, beating every full-size baseline by 1--3 points of balanced
accuracy. Under domain shift, however, in-distribution accuracy actively
misleads: EfficientViT-M0 -- the overall in-distribution winner -- is the
worst-performing model of all 12 on both external datasets, dropping below
the chance floor on Mendeley LBC (0.4731 balanced accuracy on a binary
task), while a much larger CNN baseline (ResNet-50) that is not the best
in-distribution model on any dataset is the most robust model on that same
shift. Across the full registry, CNN-family models cluster at
0.539--0.667 balanced accuracy on the severe shift condition while eight of
nine transformer-family models cluster at 0.293--0.523 (with one
transformer landing 0.003 above the lowest CNN -- a real, reported
exception, not a clean partition), and one model (MobileViT-S) collapses
to an AUROC of 0.215 -- actively anti-correlated with ground truth, not
merely uninformative -- while its own architectural successor
(MobileViTv2) shows no such failure and is in fact the strongest model on
the milder shift condition. No single static property we measured --
parameter count, FLOPs, in-distribution accuracy, or architecture
family/lineage -- reliably predicts which model fails safely under domain
shift.

\keywords{Cervical cytology \and Vision transformers \and Domain shift \and Model efficiency \and Trustworthy medical AI}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Cervical cancer remains substantially more curable when caught at a
localized stage, yet screening access is documented to be uneven across
rural and urban U.S. counties, and a growing literature proposes vision
transformers -- often marketed as ``efficient'' or ``lightweight''
variants -- as classification backbones for automated cytology screening.
Two questions are almost never asked together in that literature: does
the claimed efficiency actually cost accuracy on this specific task, and
does whatever backbone wins an in-distribution benchmark remain
trustworthy when the input distribution shifts, as it inevitably will
across institutions, scanners, staining protocols, and patient
populations? This paper answers both, using a single consistent benchmark
across a 12-model registry spanning three architecture families (standard
CNNs, full-size vision transformers, and efficient/mobile-oriented vision
transformers), evaluated first in distribution and then zero-shot on two
independent external cytology datasets.

Our contributions are threefold. First, a 12-model efficiency/accuracy
benchmark on cervical cytology classification showing that, on this task,
the most parameter-efficient models in the registry are also the most
accurate -- efficiency costs nothing here, contrary to the usual framing.
Second, a systematic escalation from a 2-model to a 4-model to a full
12-model cross-dataset domain-shift evaluation, each stage refining rather
than simply confirming the prior stage's finding, converging on: no single
static model property predicts shift robustness, and the in-distribution
winner is the shift-robustness loser. Third, a specific, load-bearing
counterexample to ``architecture family predicts behavior'' reasoning:
MobileViT-S collapses to below-chance, actively anti-correlated
predictions on one external dataset, while its direct architectural
successor, MobileViTv2, shows no such failure and is the single
best-performing model in the registry on the other external dataset.

\section{Related Work}

Vision transformer variants proposed for medical image classification
increasingly claim efficiency benefits, but rarely combine an
efficiency-budgeted architecture with cross-dataset robustness evaluation
in one study. Adjacent recent work on cervical and oral cancer imaging
includes hybrid CNN--transformer explainability models, ensemble methods
combining calibration with post-hoc explanation, and conformal-prediction
approaches to cervical screening uncertainty, none of which jointly
evaluate efficiency and real (not synthetic) cross-institution domain
shift together -- including the author's own prior work combining a
stacking ensemble with explainable AI for cervical cancer diagnosis
\cite{mohiuddin2025}, which this paper differs from by focusing on
efficiency--robustness trade-offs across a full architecture-family
registry rather than a single ensemble model.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Model registry}

Twelve models spanning three families: CNN baselines (ResNet-50
\cite{he2016resnet}, MobileNetV2 \cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2},
EfficientNet-Lite0 \cite{tan2019efficientnet}), full-size vision
transformers (ViT-Small \cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}, Swin-Tiny
\cite{liu2021swin}), and efficient/mobile-oriented vision transformers
(DeiT-Tiny \cite{touvron2021deit}, LeViT-128 \cite{graham2021levit},
TinyViT-5M \cite{wu2022tinyvit}, MobileViT-S \cite{mehta2022mobilevit},
MobileViTv2 \cite{mehta2022mobilevitv2}, EfficientViT-B0, EfficientViT-M0).
All timm tags verified against the pinned timm version rather than assumed
from memory. \emph{The EfficientViT-B0/M0 citation requires verification
before final submission: at least two unrelated architectures share the
``EfficientViT'' name in the literature (an MIT/ICCV'23 lineage and a
Microsoft/CVPR'23 lineage), and the exact source paper for the specific
timm tags used here (\texttt{efficientvit\_b0.r224\_in1k},
\texttt{efficientvit\_m0.r224\_in1k}) was not re-confirmed against the
correct lineage in this draft.}

\subsection{Training and in-distribution evaluation}

All 12 models trained on SIPaKMeD \cite{plissiti2018sipakmed}
(4,049 native-resolution single-cell crops derived directly from the
dataset's own cytoplasm-polygon boundary annotations, not a pre-resized
third-party mirror), 60/15/10/15 train/val/calibration/test split, seed 13,
full 30-epoch budget with early stopping (patience 7), on a single GPU.
Reported: balanced accuracy, macro F1, AUROC, parameter count, and
measured (not estimated) inference latency.

\subsection{Cross-dataset domain-shift evaluation}

Each model evaluated zero-shot -- no retraining, no recalibration -- on
Herlev and Mendeley LBC after harmonizing all three datasets' differing
label taxonomies onto one shared binary normal/abnormal label. This tests
generalization under the conditions this application will actually face:
different institutions, scanners, and staining protocols, not a synthetic
corruption of the same source images. \emph{Citations for the Herlev and
Mendeley LBC datasets require verification before final submission; they
are not independently confirmed beyond the data-mirror pages used for
acquisition.}

\subsection{Cross-dataset domain shift, escalating from 2 to 12 models}

\textbf{Stage 1 (DeiT-Tiny alone):} in distribution 0.9504 balanced
accuracy, Herlev (zero-shot) 0.7151, Mendeley LBC (zero-shot) 0.5230 -- a
model that looks excellent in distribution degrades to near-chance on the
more severe external dataset, with nothing in a standard pipeline able to
flag this.

\textbf{Stage 2 (adding EfficientViT-M0):} the \emph{better}
in-distribution model (0.9737 vs.\ DeiT-Tiny's 0.9504) is the
\emph{worse} model under both shifts (Herlev 0.6716 vs.\ 0.7151; Mendeley
LBC 0.4731 vs.\ 0.5230 -- the first model in this study to drop below the
0.50 chance floor). Two models this close in architecture family and
parameter count producing this reversal is a direct caution against using
in-distribution accuracy as a proxy for deployment robustness.

A near-clean CNN-vs-transformer split emerges on Mendeley LBC, with one
honest exception: all three CNNs land at 0.539--0.667; eight of nine
transformer-family models land at 0.293--0.523; the ninth (MobileViTv2)
scores 0.5418, narrowly \emph{above} the lowest CNN (0.5391) by 0.003 -- a
real overlap, reported as such rather than rounded away. This split does
not hold at all on Herlev, where transformer models occupy both the best
(MobileViTv2, TinyViT-5M) and among the weaker (LeViT-128) positions.

MobileViT-S is a severe, singular outlier: its Mendeley LBC AUROC (0.215)
is not merely below chance but actively anti-correlated with ground
truth. Its direct successor, MobileViTv2, shows no such problem and is the
single strongest model in the entire registry on Herlev (0.7501) -- the
same architecture-family name spanning a catastrophic failure and a top
performer, ruling out architecture lineage as a predictor of shift
robustness as thoroughly as parameter count and in-distribution accuracy
already have been.

\section{Discussion}

Three findings, read together, argue against three different intuitive
heuristics for selecting a deployment backbone from an in-distribution
benchmark alone. First, ``smaller is worse'' is directly falsified in
distribution (Section~4.1). Second, ``the in-distribution winner is the
safe choice'' is directly falsified under shift (Section~4.2, Stage~2):
the model with the best in-distribution accuracy in the entire study is
the worst-performing model under both external shifts, dropping below the
chance floor on one. Third, ``architecture family/lineage predicts
robustness'' is falsified by MobileViT-S and MobileViTv2 sharing a name
and a lineage while producing a catastrophic failure and a top
performance, respectively, on the same evaluation. The practical
implication is direct: a deployment decision based on an in-distribution
leaderboard, however carefully measured, cannot be assumed to transfer to
real cross-institution conditions, and no static property measured here
substitutes for actually testing under shift. This motivates the
uncertainty-quantification question our companion paper asks: since no
backbone choice alone is safe, can a model's own confidence signal at
least correctly flag when it has entered a regime where its predictions
should not be trusted?

\section{Limitations}

Two external datasets, both drawn from a small number of source
institutions; the shift conditions tested here are real but not
exhaustive of the shift a deployed system might encounter. One
train/val/calibration/test split (seed 13) per model; results are not
averaged over multiple seeds, so small differences between closely-ranked
models (e.g., the 0.003 MobileViTv2/MobileNetV2 gap on Mendeley LBC)
should be read with appropriate caution about seed-to-seed variance, which
was not separately measured. No trained-weight checkpoints persist;
reproducing any single row of these tables means retraining from scratch.

\section{Conclusion}

Twelve models across three architecture families show that, on this
cervical cytology classification task, parameter-efficient vision
transformers do not trade accuracy for efficiency -- the smallest models
in the registry are also the most accurate in distribution. But that same
efficiency-and-accuracy leaderboard actively misleads about deployment
robustness: the overall in-distribution winner is the worst-performing
model under real cross-dataset shift on both external datasets tested,
and no static property we measured reliably predicts which model fails
safely. Robustness must be measured directly, under real shift, and
cannot be inferred from an in-distribution benchmark however thorough.
