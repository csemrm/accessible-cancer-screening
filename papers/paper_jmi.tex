\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}\ \textbf{Purpose:}
Lightweight vision transformers are proposed for automated cervical cytology screening, but ``trustworthy AI'' is usually evaluated as one property that a pipeline has or lacks. We tested whether accuracy, cross-dataset robustness, explainability, selective prediction, and deployment cost agree with one another on one single-cell cervical cytology classification task, using a common model registry but different backbones for different properties.
\textbf{Approach:}
We benchmarked 12 convolutional, full-size transformer, and efficient transformer models on SIPaKMeD (five classes) and evaluated all 12 zero-shot on three external cytology datasets by mapping their outputs to a binary normal/abnormal label: Herlev (single-cell crops, the primary external test) and Mendeley LBC and APCEM (multi-cell fields of view, the latter algorithmically enhanced, treated as content-shift stress tests), so the external evaluation combines differences in institution, scale, content, preprocessing, and annotation. On a standard vision transformer, we compared three explainability methods against deletion/insertion faithfulness and expert-annotated nucleus boundaries, tested a combined confidence and conformal-set-size abstention rule, and measured parameters, floating-point operations (FLOPs), central processing unit (CPU) latency, and explanation cost.
\textbf{Results:}
The two smallest models (about 2.1M parameters) were the two most accurate in distribution in the primary run and ranked second and third by three-seed mean, on a cell-level split in which most test cells share a source image with training cells, which could inflate in-distribution scores; a cluster-grouped re-run changed balanced accuracy by a median of $-0.9$ points across the 12 models (range $-14.6$ to $+1.1$, the extreme being a training failure), comparable to seed variation. In-distribution accuracy did not predict robustness consistently: the in-distribution leader ranked 10th of 12 on both Herlev and Mendeley LBC in the primary run, and on APCEM (multi-cell fields) 9 of 12 models fell below chance on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Rank correlations between three-seed mean external accuracy and in-distribution accuracy, parameter count, and FLOPs were weak to moderate and changed sign across datasets, and model rankings changed across training seeds and datasets. Relevancy propagation was the most faithful explainability method on all three metrics on two backbones, with paired cluster-bootstrap intervals excluding zero in all 12 comparisons over 596 test cells (nucleus concentration 3.8 times chance versus 1.3 for attention rollout on DeiT-Tiny), although concentration on the nucleus does not establish clinical validity. Combining confidence with conformal set size helped only under severe shift (risk-coverage area under the curve 0.286 versus 0.324) and hurt in distribution and under mild shift. FLOPs did not predict CPU latency, and an explanation cost about 3.3 times a bare prediction.
\textbf{Conclusions:}
Accuracy, robustness, explainability, abstention, and efficiency resolved differently across experiments on the same task, so each needs its own test under the conditions of intended deployment; whether a joint test on a single backbone would change these conclusions was not tested.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} cervical cytology, vision transformers, cross-dataset generalization, explainability, selective prediction, model efficiency.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cervical cancer remains a significant cause of cancer morbidity and mortality worldwide\cite{sung2021} and is substantially more curable when detected at a localized stage, yet access to screening and specialist cytology capacity are limited in many settings. % TODO(author): cite sources for the screening-access and specialist-capacity statement A growing literature proposes vision transformers (ViTs), often marketed as efficient or lightweight variants, as classification backbones for automated cytology screening. Such a tool is only useful if it fails visibly rather than silently: a model that misclassifies confidently under conditions its training data did not cover is more dangerous than one that abstains.

``Trustworthy AI'' is usually evaluated as if it were a single property. In practice a screening pipeline makes at least five separable claims: (i) a lightweight model gives up little accuracy; (ii) its accuracy transfers across institutions, scanners, and staining protocols; (iii) its explanations are faithful; (iv) its confidence supports a reliable abstention rule; and (v) its efficiency claim holds under real measurement. A reader might reasonably assume that these resolve alike, so that testing one is enough. We test them side by side on one cervical cytology classification task and ask whether they do. The experiments share a dataset family and a 12-model registry, but, as Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations} states, they do not all use the same backbone: the benchmark covers 12 models, the explainability and abstention experiments use DeiT-Tiny, and the degradation experiment uses EfficientViT-M0.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Efficiency--accuracy benchmark.} A 12-model benchmark on SIPaKMeD spanning convolutional neural network (CNN), full-size vision transformer, and efficient vision transformer families, in which the two most parameter-efficient models are also the most accurate in distribution.
\item \textbf{Domain-shift evaluation.} A zero-shot evaluation of all 12 models on three independently sourced external datasets (Herlev, Mendeley LBC, APCEM), together with a three-seed sensitivity check, showing that in-distribution accuracy, parameter count, floating-point operations (FLOPs), and architecture family did not predict which model fails safely consistently across datasets (the direction of the association changed), and that even apparent family-level patterns did not survive a third dataset or a change of seed.
\item \textbf{Explainability and abstention.} A comparison of relevancy propagation, attention rollout, and Grad-CAM against behavioral faithfulness and expert-annotated nucleus boundaries, replicated on a second backbone, and a test of whether combining temperature-scaled confidence with conformal prediction-set size improves selective prediction.
\item \textbf{Measured deployment cost.} A measurement of parameters, FLOPs, central processing unit (CPU) latency, and explanation cost, showing that FLOPs do not predict latency and that returning an explanation costs roughly 3.3 times a bare prediction.
\end{enumerate}
The properties resolve differently: explainability method choice resolves cleanly, abstention composition resolves conditionally on shift severity, and robustness and efficiency resolve only under direct measurement. This divergence across experiments, rather than any single result, is the central observation. Because the backbones differ between experiments, the study shows that these properties are not interchangeable, but it does not show that a joint test on a single backbone is necessary to see this.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Efficient Vision Transformers}
Efficiency-oriented transformer variants fall into several loose lineages. Hybrid local--global designs (MobileViT\cite{mehta2022mobilevit}, MobileViTv2\cite{mehta2022mobilevitv2}) interleave convolutional stages with transformer blocks. Distillation-oriented designs (DeiT\cite{touvron2021deit}) keep a standard ViT backbone\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} but reach competitive accuracy with far less pretraining data. Hardware-aware designs (LeViT\cite{graham2021levit}, TinyViT\cite{wu2022tinyvit}) reintroduce convolutional stages to improve real-device latency rather than FLOP count alone. EfficientViT-M0 and EfficientViT-B0, the two smallest models in our registry, are often assumed to share a lineage because \texttt{timm} groups their tags; verified directly, they do not. EfficientViT-M0 uses cascaded group attention\cite{liu2023efficientvit}, whereas EfficientViT-B0 belongs to an unrelated lineage built on multi-scale linear attention for dense prediction\cite{cai2023efficientvit}. These design papers do not evaluate cross-institution domain shift as part of their efficiency claims, so efficiency and robustness are treated as unrelated properties. Parameter count, FLOPs, and measured speed are also known to disagree as cost indicators in mobile and efficient network design,\cite{dehghani2022,mehta2022mobilevit,vasu2023mobileone} although we found no study of this disagreement for cytology models.

\subsection{Domain Shift and Model Selection in Medical Imaging}
A general medical-imaging literature documents that models selected on held-out in-distribution data can degrade under real distribution shift across scanners, sites, and populations,\cite{zech2018,guan2022} motivating external validation. Benchmarks of real-world distribution shift,\cite{koh2021} work on shortcut learning,\cite{geirhos2020} and work on underspecification\cite{damour2020} show why in-distribution accuracy can be a poor guide: models that perform equivalently on held-out data can behave differently once the data change. Predictive uncertainty also degrades under shift.\cite{ovadia2019} In cervical cytology, recent studies have examined classifier behavior on real-world and multi-scanner data,\cite{ferreira2026,coskun2026} and external validation across independent cervical cytology datasets has been described as rare.\cite{albzour2026hybrid} That literature typically evaluates one or a few architectures. We instead sweep CNN, full-size transformer, and efficient transformer families at matched task and data scale and ask whether any measurable static model property predicts which model degrades least.

\subsection{Data Splitting and Source-Image Overlap}
Splitting at the level of images, tiles, or records when several samples come from the same subject can inflate accuracy relative to subject-level splitting, as described in digital pathology,\cite{bussola2021} clinical machine learning,\cite{saeb2017} and brain MRI.\cite{rumala2023} For SIPaKMeD, Albzour and Lam note that the cropped-cell filenames encode the parent cluster image and that parent-level grouping was not enforced in their cross-validation, and they treat this as a limitation without measuring it.\cite{albzour2026hybrid} We measure the overlap under the split used here (Sec.~\ref{sec:split}) but do not estimate its effect on accuracy.

\subsection{Transformer Explainability and Faithfulness}
Attention rollout\cite{abnar2020quantifying} chains attention weights across layers as a relevance signal, a premise contested in the literature on whether attention is an explanation.\cite{jain2019,wiegreffe2019} Relevancy propagation\cite{chefer2021transformer} weights each block's attention by its gradient with respect to the target class, making the map class-discriminative. Grad-CAM\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} was designed for convolutional feature maps and is commonly adapted to transformers by targeting a block's token activations. Deletion/insertion faithfulness\cite{petsiuk2018rise} measures the effect of removing or revealing the regions an explanation marks as important on the model's own prediction. Evaluating localization against SIPaKMeD's expert-marked nucleus polygons avoids compounding the evaluation with a second model's errors. Prior studies have evaluated explanation methods for medical vision transformers, including Grad-CAM and attention-based methods;\cite{barekatain2026,chung2025,komorowski2023} we did not find prior work in our search that scores explanations against expert nucleus masks in cervical cytology.

\subsection{Selective Prediction and Calibration}
Temperature scaling\cite{guo2017calibration} calibrates confidence on a held-out split. Split conformal prediction gives a distribution-free coverage guarantee from a single calibration split,\cite{angelopoulos2023} and its prediction-set size is a model-agnostic but coarse uncertainty proxy. MC Dropout\cite{gal2016dropout}, deep ensembles\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, and evidential deep learning\cite{sensoy2018evidential} are alternative uncertainty signals that we do not evaluate here. Conformal prediction has been applied to cervical atypia classification,\cite{hagos2026} and calibration with selective prediction has been studied for Pap smear classification.\cite{albzour2026selective} In our search we did not find work that combines confidence with conformal set size for abstention under cross-dataset shift in cytology, which is the question we examine.

\subsection{Adjacent Cervical and Clinical-AI Work}
Comparisons of many convolutional and transformer architectures on cytology images,\cite{liu2021aspect} ensembles that include EfficientViT on SIPaKMeD and Herlev,\cite{lima2025} and systematic evaluations of vision transformers for cervical classification\cite{albzour2026cancers} exist; among them we did not identify an evaluation of zero-shot transfer across independent datasets, judging from their titles and abstracts. % TODO(author): read these three papers to confirm before submission
Adjacent work includes hybrid deep feature fusion for cervical cell classification\cite{rahaman2021} and a stacking-ensemble approach with explainable AI for cervical cancer diagnosis,\cite{mohiuddin2025} which we differ from by evaluating explainability against expert-annotated localization and behavioral faithfulness and by jointly evaluating efficiency and real cross-institution shift. A lightweight transformer with cross-scale attention and explainability for plant disease diagnosis\cite{rahman2026maizeformerx} motivates our treatment of efficiency and explainability as joint design goals, and two further papers address explainable clinical AI in other domains\cite{gony2026retinal,ahmed2026postsurgical}.

These statements reflect a non-exhaustive literature search conducted in October 2026 (Crossref, arXiv, and web search); the absence of a prior study in our search does not establish that none exists.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}
All data are from publicly available, previously published image datasets, and no new patient data were collected. All models were trained on SIPaKMeD\cite{plissiti2018sipakmed}: 4,049 native-resolution single-cell crops derived from the dataset's own cytoplasm-polygon annotations, in five cytomorphology classes. External evaluation used Herlev\cite{jantzen2005herlev}, Mendeley LBC\cite{hussain2020mendeley}, and the APCEM-Enhanced Cervical Cytology Pap Smear Image Dataset\cite{aswini2026apcem} (4,253 images in the six-class Bethesda taxonomy, with dataset class labels NILM, ASC-US, ASC-H, LSIL, HSIL, and SSC). Because the four datasets use different label taxonomies, external evaluation used one shared binary normal/abnormal label (Table~\ref{tab:labels}). Models were trained on SIPaKMeD's five classes, and each model's five-class softmax output was summed into normal and abnormal probabilities for external evaluation (Sec.~\ref{sec:shift}). The external datasets differ from SIPaKMeD and from each other in acquisition pipeline, institution, and label granularity, and the correspondence between their diagnostic categories is an assumption that the dataset documentation does not establish. We therefore describe the external evaluation as cross-dataset generalization under combined acquisition and annotation differences, and we use ``domain shift'' in this sense throughout; the experiments do not separate acquisition differences from label-definition differences. Each external dataset is an independent test rather than a synthetic corruption of the same source images. The datasets also differ in what an image contains (Table~\ref{tab:units}): SIPaKMeD and Herlev images are crops of single cells, whereas Mendeley LBC and APCEM images show many cells in a field of view, and the APCEM images were produced by an algorithmic enhancement procedure applied by the dataset authors. All images were resized without cropping to each model's square input size, which changes the aspect ratio of the non-square Mendeley LBC and Herlev images. A classifier trained on single cells is therefore applied to whole fields in two of the three external evaluations. We treat Herlev, which preserves the single-cell task, as the primary external evaluation, and Mendeley LBC and APCEM as content-shift stress tests rather than as controlled cross-institution tests. The harmonized external sets are imbalanced (Herlev 74\% abnormal, APCEM 82\% abnormal, Mendeley LBC 36\% abnormal, SIPaKMeD 40\% abnormal), which is why balanced accuracy is reported.

\subsection{Model Registry}
\label{sec:registry}
Twelve models spanning three families were evaluated: CNN baselines (ResNet-50\cite{he2016resnet}, MobileNetV2\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}, EfficientNet-Lite0\cite{tan2019efficientnet}); full-size vision transformers (ViT-Small\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}, Swin-Tiny\cite{liu2021swin}); and efficient or mobile-oriented vision transformers (DeiT-Tiny\cite{touvron2021deit}, LeViT-128\cite{graham2021levit}, TinyViT-5M\cite{wu2022tinyvit}, MobileViT-S\cite{mehta2022mobilevit}, MobileViTv2\cite{mehta2022mobilevitv2}, EfficientViT-B0\cite{cai2023efficientvit}, EfficientViT-M0\cite{liu2023efficientvit}). All \texttt{timm} tags were verified to exist with pretrained ImageNet weights, and the two EfficientViT variants' source papers were confirmed against \texttt{timm}'s model cards rather than inferred from the shared tag prefix (exact library versions were not recorded; Table~\ref{tab:repro}).

\subsection{Training and In-Distribution Evaluation}
\label{sec:training}
Models were trained on SIPaKMeD's five classes from ImageNet-pretrained weights with the AdamW optimizer (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), a cosine learning-rate schedule over 30 epochs, cross-entropy loss, batch size 32, and early stopping on validation loss (patience 7); the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss was retained. Input resolution followed each model's \texttt{timm} pretrained configuration, with ImageNet normalization. Training augmentation comprised random horizontal and vertical flips, random rotation of up to 20 degrees, and brightness and contrast jitter of 0.15. Training used a single graphics processing unit (GPU; Table~\ref{tab:repro}). We report balanced accuracy, macro F1, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and parameter count. For the five-class in-distribution evaluation, balanced accuracy has a chance level of 0.20, whereas for the binary external evaluation it has a chance level of 0.50, so the two are not on the same scale and are compared only by rank. Evaluation stages were run as separate training and evaluation runs (see Sec.~\ref{sec:runvar}).

\subsubsection{Data split and source-image overlap}
\label{sec:split}
SIPaKMeD was split 60/15/10/15 into training, validation, calibration, and test sets. The split is stratified by class (shuffled within each class with seed 13 plus the class index) and is performed at the level of single-cell crops. The 4,049 cells come from 966 cluster images (about 4.2 cells per image), and cells from one cluster image were not kept together. Reproducing the split procedure on the crop filenames shows that 533 of the 607 test cells (87.8\%) share a source cluster image with at least one training cell. Patient and slide identifiers are not available, so even cluster-level separation would not guarantee patient-level independence. In-distribution results, including in-distribution calibration, abstention, and degradation results, may therefore be optimistic and should not be read as an estimate of performance on new patients; Sec.~\ref{sec:cluster} reports a cluster-grouped re-run that gauges the size of this effect. The external-dataset evaluations are not affected by this overlap, because no external image was used for training, validation, or calibration. The same split manifest was used at all training seeds.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Domain-Shift Evaluation}
\label{sec:shift}
Each model was evaluated zero-shot on Herlev, Mendeley LBC, and APCEM, with no retraining or recalibration, by summing its five-class softmax output into normal and abnormal probabilities according to Table~\ref{tab:labels}. To measure how much individual rankings depend on the training run, all 12 models were additionally retrained at two further seeds (17 and 29) and evaluated zero-shot on all three external datasets from a single checkpoint per model per seed.

\subsection{Explainability Backbone and Methods}
\label{sec:xai-methods}
Relevancy propagation and attention rollout are defined for standard multi-head self-attention blocks, and EfficientViT-M0 has no established propagation rule for either. Rather than force an ad hoc extension, the explainability and abstention experiments use \textbf{DeiT-Tiny}, a standard \texttt{timm} \texttt{VisionTransformer} that reached 0.9606 balanced accuracy in the benchmark (Sec.~\ref{sec:bench}), retrained for these experiments. All three methods produce a relevance map over the 14$\times$14 patch grid (224-pixel input, 16-pixel patches), upsampled to image resolution.
\emph{Attention rollout}: raw softmax attention averaged over heads and chained across all 12 blocks; class-agnostic.
\emph{Relevancy propagation}: the same rollout recurrence, with each block's contribution equal to $\mathrm{ReLU}(\nabla_{\text{attn}} \odot \text{attn})$ averaged over heads, where the gradient is taken with respect to the model's predicted-class logit; class-discriminative.
\emph{Grad-CAM (ViT-adapted)}: gradient-weighted channel importance applied to a transformer block's token activations. The target layer must be the \emph{second-to-last} block: DeiT-Tiny pools only the classification (CLS) token, so no layer after the final block mixes information back into patch positions. We verified this directly: every patch token's gradient at the last block's output was exactly zero, whereas the second-to-last block's patch-token gradients vary normally. Targeting the wrong block therefore yields an all-zero heatmap silently, not an error.

\subsection{Faithfulness and Localization Metrics}
\label{sec:xai-metrics}
\emph{Deletion/insertion area under the curve (AUC)}\cite{petsiuk2018rise}: patches are ranked by a method's own relevance score and progressively replaced with (deletion) or revealed from (insertion) a heavily Gaussian-blurred copy of the image, tracking the predicted-class probability; we report the area under the probability-versus-fraction curve (lower is better for deletion, higher for insertion).
\emph{Nucleus concentration ratio}: SIPaKMeD provides expert-marked nucleus boundary polygons per cell, which we persist in crop-local pixel coordinates. The ratio is the fraction of a heatmap's total relevance mass inside the nucleus divided by the nucleus's share of image area. A ratio of 1.0 is exactly chance, and higher values mean relevance concentrates in the nucleus more than chance would predict, which is clinically the correct place to concentrate.

\subsection{Calibration, Conformal Prediction, and the Combined Abstention Signal}
\label{sec:selective-methods}
Temperature scaling\cite{guo2017calibration} was fit on a calibration split disjoint from training and test data. Conformal prediction sets used the nonconformity score one minus the softmax probability of the true class, a target miscoverage of 0.1, and the calibration split (about 400 images, 10\% of SIPaKMeD, which shares source images with the training split as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:split}). The threshold was the mean, over 200 bootstrap resamples of the calibration scores (each 80\% of the calibration set, drawn with replacement), of the finite-sample-corrected split-conformal quantile at level $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$, and a set contains every class whose softmax probability is at least one minus the threshold. This averaged threshold is a stabilizing heuristic for the small calibration set and does not carry the finite-sample guarantee of a single split-conformal threshold, and the standard single split-conformal threshold was not evaluated for comparison. % TODO(author): compute the standard split-conformal variant and clinical operating points (error among accepted cases at fixed coverage) from retained predictions Under external shift the exchangeability assumption behind the coverage guarantee does not hold, so we use the prediction-set size only as an uncertainty signal and make no claim about coverage under shift. The combined selective-prediction signal rank-normalizes temperature-scaled max-softmax confidence and (inverted) conformal prediction-set size independently, then averages the ranks. Selective prediction was scored by risk-coverage AUC (lower is better) in distribution, on Herlev (moderate shift), and on Mendeley LBC (severe shift). The shift labels reflect the observed degradation of DeiT-Tiny on these datasets (Table~\ref{tab:shift12}).

\subsection{Efficiency and Latency Measurement}
\label{sec:eff-methods}
Parameters and FLOPs (via \texttt{torch.utils.flop\_counter.FlopCounterMode}, counting a multiply-add as two FLOPs) are architecture properties independent of trained weights, so they were measured without training. CPU latency (single-image batch of random input, PyTorch eager mode, median of 30 iterations after 5 warm-up iterations) and memory (isolated per model in its own subprocess) were likewise measured with untrained weights, on one shared consumer laptop. Untrained weights are appropriate because the latency of dense inference depends on the architecture and tensor shapes rather than on learned weight values. The laptop's CPU model, thread count, and library versions were not recorded (Table~\ref{tab:repro}). % TODO(author): add the laptop CPU model and thread count if known The cost of an explanation was measured as a full forward, backward, and rollout pass for relevancy propagation's own predicted-class explanation against a bare forward pass, both on CPU. Robustness to synthetic degradation (JPEG compression, Gaussian blur, brightness/contrast shift at five severities each) and a synthetic out-of-distribution (OOD) sanity check (25 images of noise, solid colors, and checkerboards) were run on a freshly trained EfficientViT-M0.

\subsection{Note on Run-to-Run Variation}
\label{sec:runvar}
In-distribution accuracy of the same model differs slightly between tables (for example, EfficientViT-M0: 0.9654 in Table~\ref{tab:bench}, 0.9737 in Table~\ref{tab:shift12}, 0.9621 in Table~\ref{tab:apcem}, and about 0.975 in the degradation experiment; all are five-class balanced accuracy). The benchmark, the Herlev and Mendeley LBC evaluation, and the APCEM evaluation each used the same recipe and seed (13), but each model was trained afresh in a separate session for each stage; ViT-Small's Herlev score, for example, was 0.7194 in a partially lost earlier run and 0.7049 in the retrained run. The training script sets only the PyTorch random seed; it does not enable deterministic cuDNN algorithms and does not separately seed NumPy, Python, or the four shuffled data-loader workers, so identically seeded runs are not expected to be bit-identical. We did not isolate which factor accounts for the differences, so we treat them as run-to-run variation of unidentified origin. The three-seed analysis (Sec.~\ref{sec:seeds}) measures sensitivity to the training seed, whereas same-seed repeatability was not measured separately. The degradation experiment additionally used a different random train/validation split. Comparisons are therefore valid within a table, not across tables.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:stats}
Per-image predictions were not retained for the original runs, so bootstrap confidence intervals could not be computed for them. The cluster-grouped re-run (Sec.~\ref{sec:cluster}) retained them, and for that run we report 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals (2,000 resamples) for balanced accuracy, resampling source cluster images for in-distribution results and individual images for external results, together with per-class sensitivity and specificity. For the original runs we instead report three things. First, the mean and standard deviation of balanced accuracy over three training seeds (13, 17, and 29) for every model and dataset (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}). Second, Spearman rank correlations across the 12 models between three-seed mean external balanced accuracy and in-distribution accuracy, parameter count, FLOPs, and architecture family (CNN versus transformer; Table~\ref{tab:rho}). With 12 models, $|\rho|\geq 0.587$ is required for two-sided $p<0.05$ without correction for the twelve comparisons, so we report these correlations descriptively. Third, conservative worst-case 95\% half-widths for test-set sampling error of a single balanced accuracy, $1.96\times 0.5\sqrt{0.25/n_{\text{normal}}+0.25/n_{\text{abnormal}}}$, which assume independent images and the largest possible variance. Image-level sampling uncertainty and training-seed variability are different sources of uncertainty and are reported separately.

\subsection{Seed Sensitivity}
\label{sec:seeds}
Variance across random seeds is a known threat to benchmark conclusions.\cite{picard2021,bouthillier2021} Each headline model was trained once at seed 13. We measured seed-to-seed variance directly for two close-ranked pairs at two further seeds (17 and 29), and then extended the check to all 12 models on all three external datasets.

\subsubsection{Close calls can go either way}
On Mendeley LBC, MobileViTv2's 0.003 edge over MobileNetV2 (0.5418 versus 0.5391) does not survive: MobileNetV2 ranges 0.493--0.604 and MobileViTv2 ranges 0.494--0.623 across the three seeds, the ranges overlap almost completely, and the ranking flips at seed 17. On APCEM, EfficientViT-B0's 0.001 edge over DeiT-Tiny (0.4481 versus 0.4469) does survive: EfficientViT-B0 ranges 0.421--0.448, DeiT-Tiny ranges 0.367--0.447, EfficientViT-B0 wins at all three seeds, and its margin widens (to 0.027, then 0.067). The same kind of close call produced opposite outcomes once tested.

\subsubsection{The Mendeley LBC family split is seed-specific}
At seed 13 only 1 of 9 transformer-family models exceeds the weakest CNN (by 0.003). At seed 17, 3 of 9 do, and at seed 29, 4 of 9 do, by margins up to 0.11.

\subsubsection{The in-distribution leader is not reliably the robustness loser}
EfficientViT-M0 is the (near-)in-distribution leader and ranks 10th of 12 on both Herlev and Mendeley LBC at seed 13. At seed 29 it is the outright in-distribution winner and mid-pack under shift on both datasets (rank 4--5 of 12), and at seed 17 it is not even the in-distribution winner (rank 10 of 12). By three-seed mean, EfficientViT-M0 ranked second in distribution but 8th, 7th, and 8th of 12 on Herlev, Mendeley LBC, and APCEM. It should be read as a seed-13 illustration rather than a reproducible property of that model.

\subsubsection{Some findings replicate cleanly}
Herlev's inverse pattern (most transformer models outscoring the CNNs) and MobileNetV2's status as the strongest model on APCEM both replicate across all three seeds.

Conservative worst-case 95\% half-widths for the balanced accuracy of a single model are $\pm 0.037$ on Herlev (242 normal, 675 abnormal images), $\pm 0.033$ on Mendeley LBC (612, 350), and $\pm 0.019$ on APCEM (779, 3,474), before accounting for training-seed variance. \subsubsection{Cost of an explanation}
On CPU, a bare DeiT-Tiny forward pass had a median latency of 49.6 ms, and a prediction that also returned a relevancy-propagation explanation cost 164.1 ms, about 3.3 times the bare-prediction cost. Both numbers use the same protocol (single-image batch, 5 warm-up iterations, median of 30), but the bare-forward and explanation pair (49.6 ms and 164.1 ms) was measured on the CPU of a cloud virtual machine during the abstention experiment, whereas Table~\ref{tab:eff} comes from the consumer laptop. The two figures are therefore not comparable in absolute terms; the 3.3 times ratio is a within-machine comparison.

\subsubsection{Synthetic degradation and OOD check}
EfficientViT-M0 degraded gradually, not catastrophically, under all three corruptions (Fig.~\ref{fig:degradation}). Gaussian blur was by far the most damaging, costing 32.8 points of balanced accuracy at its most severe level (radius 8), roughly double JPEG compression's worst case (15.5 points) and nearly double brightness/contrast's (18.1 points). A plausible reason is that Bethesda-system diagnosis depends on fine nuclear texture, which blur removes directly. On the 25-image synthetic OOD sample, mean max-softmax confidence fell from 0.985 (real in-distribution images) to 0.743, a real drop but far above the 0.20 chance level for this five-class task, so confidence thresholding alone gives only a weak OOD signal.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read together, the results argue against several intuitive heuristics for selecting and verifying a deployment pipeline from a single evaluation. \emph{``Smaller is worse''} is not supported in distribution (Sec.~\ref{sec:bench}), although these in-distribution scores come from a split with substantial source-image overlap (a cluster-grouped re-run changed them only modestly, Sec.~\ref{sec:cluster}). \emph{``The in-distribution leader is the safe choice''} is not supported: the leader ranked poorly under shift in the primary run, and even this ranking varies with the training seed (Sec.~\ref{sec:seeds}). \emph{``Architecture lineage predicts robustness''} is contradicted by MobileViT-S and MobileViTv2, which share a lineage and produced a catastrophic failure and a top result on the same evaluation. \emph{``CNNs are the robust family''} looked stable on Herlev and Mendeley LBC, but a third, independently sourced dataset (APCEM) did not support it, and a seed check showed that the Mendeley LBC split was itself seed-specific. Across the three datasets, no static property had an association with robustness that kept its direction (Table~\ref{tab:rho}). The sharpest methodological point is that a robustness pattern that survives two external datasets is not guaranteed to survive a third, so ``tested under two external shifts'' is not a licence to stop testing.

These external results measure something narrower than deployment across institutions. Herlev, the dataset closest in content to the training data (single-cell crops), produced the highest external accuracies and a consistent pattern across seeds in which most transformer models outscored the CNNs. The two field-of-view datasets, Mendeley LBC and APCEM, ask a single-cell classifier to classify whole fields, and APCEM additionally changes the image appearance by enhancement. Below-chance results on those two datasets are therefore evidence that these classifiers do not transfer to field-level images under a normal/abnormal mapping, not evidence of how they would behave on single-cell crops from another institution. The robustness rankings should be read in that light.

The other three properties behaved differently from robustness and from each other. Explainability method choice produced the cleanest result, a single winner on every metric on both evaluated backbones, which suggests that choosing the method matters for these transformer backbones; the paired intervals on 596 cells support this, although concentration on the nucleus is not a clinical validity measure. Abstention composition was conditional on shift severity. Efficiency claims required direct measurement: FLOPs did not predict latency, an explanation cost about 3.3 times a bare prediction, and OOD confidence dropped only partially. No single verification strategy (pick the best method once, assume composition helps, trust an architectural efficiency claim) would have characterized all five properties correctly.

For deployment, the practical implications are direct. A backbone should not be chosen from an in-distribution leaderboard, however carefully measured, and robustness must be measured under the shift the deployment will actually face, ideally across multiple seeds. Confidence-based abstention, even when combined with conformal set size, did not reach a reliable operating point under severe shift, so a screening tool should expect to route a substantial fraction of shifted-domain cases to specialist review. Blur is the most damaging of the tested corruptions, which makes focus quality in any handheld or low-cost capture setting a first priority for field validation.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Data.} All four datasets come from a small number of source institutions, and the three external shifts are real cross-dataset differences, not a systematic sweep of shift types (scanner resolution, stain intensity, or demographics varied independently). Because the datasets also differ in what an image contains (single cells versus fields of view), in preprocessing (APCEM enhancement), and in label definitions, the experiments do not separate institutional differences from scale, content, preprocessing, and annotation differences. The findings are demonstrated for twelve models and three external datasets; the observation that the tested static properties were not consistent predictors across datasets is the one most likely to generalize, whereas specific rankings, including which model is most robust, should not be assumed to transfer to a fourth dataset. Label harmonization to a binary task discards clinically important granularity and rests on category correspondences that the dataset documentation does not establish. SIPaKMeD (about 4,000 images) is a single-institution training source.

\textbf{Source-image overlap.} The in-distribution split is at the cell level, 87.8\% of test cells share a source cluster image with a training cell, and no patient or slide identifiers are available (Sec.~\ref{sec:split}). In-distribution accuracy, calibration, abstention, and degradation results may therefore be optimistic. A cluster-level or patient-level re-split would be needed to estimate in-distribution performance on new patients, and it could change the in-distribution rankings reported here. The external-dataset results do not depend on this split. A single cluster-grouped re-run (Sec.~\ref{sec:cluster}) changed in-distribution balanced accuracy by a median of $-0.9$ points, comparable to seed variation, so it does not show a large inflation; however, that comparison is unpaired and uses one split, and a valid estimate needs a paired design (for example, repeated cell-level and cluster-grouped cross-validation on the same cells, with variability across which clusters fall in the test set). Earlier work has compared record-wise and subject-wise splitting on other medical datasets.\cite{saeb2017,rumala2023}

\textbf{Seeds, runs, and statistics.} Each headline result is a single run at seed 13 on one data split. Seed variance was measured for all 12 models at two further seeds, and it showed that several single-model and single-dataset claims are seed-specific (the Mendeley LBC family split and EfficientViT-M0 as the in-distribution winner that fails under shift), so individual rows and close-ranked comparisons should be read with caution. Different tables come from separate runs of unidentified run-to-run variation (Sec.~\ref{sec:runvar}), and same-seed repeatability was not measured. Per-image predictions and trained checkpoints were not retained for the original runs, so bootstrap confidence intervals, per-class sensitivity and specificity, and confusion matrices are available only for the cluster-grouped re-run; for the original runs we report seed statistics, rank correlations, and conservative sampling bounds (Sec.~\ref{sec:stats}). The combined-signal abstention experiment was not repeated on the re-run. Reproducing a row means retraining from scratch, and exact software versions were not recorded (Table~\ref{tab:repro}).

\textbf{Backbone mismatch.} The five properties were not all measured on one backbone: the benchmark and efficiency analysis cover 12 models, the explainability and abstention experiments use DeiT-Tiny (with ViT-Small for explainability), and the degradation and out-of-distribution experiments use EfficientViT-M0. DeiT-Tiny was used for explainability and abstention, not the benchmark's in-distribution leader, because relevancy propagation is not defined for EfficientViT-M0. The central observation therefore concerns differences across experiments, and we did not test whether evaluating all properties on one backbone would change it. The explainability ranking was confirmed on one additional same-family backbone (ViT-Small), but the abstention-composition result and the degradation and OOD findings were not re-tested on it. Whether any of these results transfer to a hybrid convolution--attention backbone, or to a different imaging task, is untested.

\textbf{Measurement.} The original explainability comparison used 50 cells and retained only means; the full-sample re-evaluation uses all 596 cluster-grouped test cells with paired intervals, but only for two related plain-transformer backbones, one deletion/insertion configuration (20 steps, blurred baseline), and one run. Deletion and insertion rankings can depend on the replacement baseline, and the nucleus concentration ratio measures overlap with an annotated nucleus, not clinical validity or reader agreement. The OOD check uses a small synthetic sample (25 images) rather than a natural out-of-domain dataset. CPU latency was measured on one shared consumer laptop (about 30\% run-to-run swing) rather than dedicated or clinic-grade hardware, so the 3.3 times explanation-cost multiplier should be read as approximate. Accuracy, macro F1, and AUROC are standard metrics, which reduces the risk that the findings are artifacts of an unusual metric.

\textbf{Clinical validity.} No clinical or expert-review validation of explanation fidelity was performed. Post hoc explanations offer limited assurance about a model's reasoning unless they are validated,\cite{ghassemi2021} and agreement with a nucleus boundary is not equivalent to a cytotechnologist judging an explanation clinically meaningful. No prospective or reader-study evaluation of the full pipeline was performed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Five properties of single-cell cervical cytology classification, tested across experiments on a common dataset family and model registry, did not resolve alike. Parameter-efficient vision transformers did not trade accuracy for efficiency in distribution, yet the in-distribution leaderboard misled about robustness: none of the static properties we measured predicted robustness consistently across the three external datasets, and even apparent family-level patterns did not survive a third external dataset or a change of training seed. In-distribution scores came from a cell-level split with substantial source-image overlap and are likely optimistic. Relevancy propagation was more faithful than attention rollout or Grad-CAM on behavioral faithfulness and on expert-annotated localization on both evaluated transformer backbones. Combining two uncertainty signals for abstention helped only under the severe shift where the better individual signal was already failing. Efficiency claims held only under direct measurement, since FLOPs did not predict latency and an explanation cost roughly 3.3 times a bare prediction. Each property needed its own test, on its own terms, under the conditions of intended deployment.
